Designing and implementing the BBNJ Clearing-House Mechanism (CHM): Contribution from the Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission 
Technical Considerations and Operational Framework for the BBNJ Clearing-House Mechanism 
Information for the Preparatory Commission for the BBNJ Agreement, Third Session (New York, 23 March - 2 April 2026) 
1. Executive Overview
The BBNJ Agreement establishes a Clearing-House Mechanism (CHM) as its central information infrastructure to serve all four treaty pillars: marine genetic resources (MGR), area-based management tools (ABMTs), environmental impact assessments (EIAs), and capacity-building and transfer of marine technology (CB/TMT). Under Article 51, the Secretariat is mandated to manage the CHM, but may cooperate with relevant international organizations, including the IOC, ISA, IMO and FAO. The Agreement entered into force on 17 January 2026, making rapid CHM development urgent. The Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission (IOC) of UNESCO is well-positioned to serve as a technical partner, building on six decades of operational ocean data infrastructure, global ocean science networks of national and regional bodies, and a focus on building technical and scientific capacities of nations. 
2. Defining the CHM: Information, Not Just Data
A critical distinction for BBNJ Parties is that the CHM can be understood as an information management system, not a data management system.
	•	What it is NOT: A central repository replicating the function of existing global ocean data repositories, which would be redundant and costly.
	•	What it IS: A “system-of-systems” that manages regulatory workflows, notifications, roles and privileges, and access to information held in existing, high-quality global repositories and served through existing platforms. Specifically, it manages:
	•	regulatory workflows (notifications, reviews and audit logs, version controls, approvals, and deadlines); 
	•	identifiers (issuing and resolving Standardized Batch Identifiers that trace MGR from collection to benefit-sharing); 
	•	access to information (a single entry point to discover and interlink information in federated systems); and 
	•	metadata and provenance (ensuring all information is findable, attributable and traceable).
It does not need to build a global ocean database but could connect to existing systems through standardised, interoperable access services.
3. Designing the BBNJ CHM 
Based on IOC’s technical experience, a modular and phased approach would ensure the CHM is operational as soon as possible :
	•	Component 1: Central Platform for Governance and Control. This is a purpose-built "regulatory core" that could be built by IOC (or a subcontractor) managed by the BBNJ Secretariat (see following text on how the IOC suggests this working in practice). It would handle functions unique to the BBNJ Agreement, such as:
	•	MGR Notifications: Processing pre-, post-collection and utilization notifications and issuing Standardized Batch Identifiers (SBIs) to report on the MGR lifecycle.
	•	Workflow Management: Facilitating submissions of ABMT proposals, EIA screening, and CB/TT "matchmaking" between needs and offers.
	•	Component 2: Federated Interoperable Access Service This layer connects the central platform to existing data systems, which would provide data required for e.g. ABMT proposals, or EIA reports. Such an approach would avoid duplication and deliver efficiency for users through linkage to already adopted platforms and mechanisms. It leverages access to a multitude of existing databases including:
	•	OBIS (Ocean Biodiversity Information System): 170 million+ marine biodiversity records and global standard protocols for handling marine biodiversity data, managed and operated by IOC.
	•	ODIS (Ocean Data and Information System): IOC’s existing federated data discovery architecture currently used across Europe, Australia, USA, Africa, Latin America, and the Pacific.
	•	Scientific Databases: Direct links to genetic sequence repositories (INSDC - International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration) and expert rosters (e.g. ORCID – Open Research and Contributor ID – linked to IOC’s OceanExpert).
4. Implementing the BBNJ CHM 
	•	Operational Readiness: The CHM needs immediate operational readiness to fulfill its basic functions and the potential to be fine-tuned over time. In a phased approach, a first component would ensure meeting immediate needs, while the connections to a comprehensive network of existing databases can be developed over time. This requires linkages to existing data infrastructures and continuous access to scientific and technical know-how. The IOC can support this with experience in managing data and information products (such as IODE/OBIS), ocean observations (e.g. GOOS) and training platforms (e.g. OTGA) and with long-term scientific and technical advice through its networks across 152 Member States as well as through the IOC BBNJ Working Group. 
	•	SBI Development: A standardized BBNJ Batch Identifier will need to be developed within a short timeframe to capture notifications in regards to activities related to MGRs of ABNJ and enable the operationalisation of equitable benefit-sharing. Input by the scientific community is crucial in the development of the SBI to put the treaty’s objectives into practice. IOC can support with dedicated expertise needed to set up a Standardized Batch Identifier system, e.g. through a Digital Object Identifier (DOI)-based approach, where each batch of collected marine genetic resources receives a DOI assigning each MGR batch a globally unique DOI, the standard already used across scientific publishing and data repositories, ensuring each collection is permanently findable and traceable, which would provide persistence, machine-resolvability, and interoperability with research data infrastructure (DataCite, INSDC, GBIF – Global Biodiversity Information Facility, OBIS). Such an approach would be compatible with the CBD’s IRCC system under the Nagoya Protocol, ensuring coherence across jurisdictional boundaries. 
	•	Global Network Reach: The BBNJ CHM is global in nature: It serves States and stakeholders from across the world and is the entry point for BBNJ information, including MGR notifications, ABMT proposals, EIA reports, or CBTMT initiatives. At the same time it is regionally grounded through the need to connect to different regions and stakeholders for ABMT and EIA alerts, and CB/TMT matchmaking. IOC has valuable experience in connecting national and regional bodies with global initiatives such as through operating 115 centres across 67 countries, of which 62 National Oceanographic Data Centres, 36 OBIS nodes and 17 Regional Training Centres (annex C), and through its network of national focal points for Ocean Observing (part of the Global Ocean Observing System and Oceanographic Data Management (through the International Oceanographic Data and Information Exchange Committee in nearly 90 countries. Nations could build upon and strengthen these national and regional centres to support the BBNJ implementation.
5. Cooperating for a BBNJ CHM 
Article 51(4) of the BBNJ Agreement refers to the IOC as a potential cooperation partner in the development of the BBNJ CHM. In the case IOC would be solicited to act as technical developer of the CHM, a cooperation mechanism would need to be designed to reflect the BBNJ Secretariat responsibilities in terms of management of the CHM and the role of IOC in terms of development and implementation of the CHM. Potential responsibilities could be defined as follows:
	•	BBNJ Secretariat: Enacts policy, oversees coordination of operations, and maintains authority over standards and procedures.
	•	IOC: Acts as a technical partner, developing, hosting and maintaining the infrastructure under a formal cooperation arrangement.
	•	Parties: Maintain data sovereignty through their national data infrastructures and/or regional nodes (for eg regional data  that would be linked to the CHM, remaining the authoritative sources of truth for their data in the region).
	•	COP: Determines operational modalities (Art. 51(2)), provides strategic direction, and approves cooperation arrangements.
This model would follow a proven precedent: OBIS operates under IOC governance, with a centrally managed platform (data harvesting, quality control, unified API) while 36 nodes maintain data sovereignty within countries. “Manage” in a treaty context refers to governance, coordination and standard-setting, and not necessarily to centralised hosting of all technical infrastructure.
6. Possible considerations for PrepCom-3
Early development of the CHM is essential. We suggest that delegations consider:
	•	Formally recognizing IOC-UNESCO as technical partner for CHM development.
	•	Mandating a CHM Prototype during the preparatory period (pre-COP) to deliver a "Minimum Viable Product" focusing on the regulatory core (Component 1).
	•	Inviting a Technical Roadmap from IOC, including cost estimates for a fully operational system, to be presented to the COP-1.
	•	Supporting Development through specific allocated financial resources (e.g. through the Special Fund or voluntary contributions - or other mechanisms upon PrepCom or COP decision - to ensure the CHM is ready to facilitate treaty implementation within a short time frame, no longer than 24 months after COP-1.
	•	Requesting the interim Secretariat, in cooperation with IOC, to begin technical design consultations on CHM architecture, standards, interoperability and budget requirements.
